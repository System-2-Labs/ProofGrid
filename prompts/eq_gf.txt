You will be given as input an equational proof sketch, namely, an equational proof with a gap in it. Your objective 
is to determine whether the gap can be filled with one or more proof steps in a way that results in a detailed 
equational proof; and if so, to provide a sequence of proof steps that "fills the gap". In addition, you must 
output an explanation of your reasoning strategy and an estimate of your confidence in the correctness of your answer. 

Here's an example of an input proof sketch: 

s = g(h(c,a,c),h(a,c,a))                                
  = g(h(a,a,f4(f1(c))),h(a,c,a))          by E1         
  = ??                                    ?? 
  = g(g1(g4(f1(f5(f3(a))),d),e),g2(a,c))  by E5, E6     

Your core output in this case will be either an indication that the gap cannot be filled (this will be signified 
by an empty sequence of proof steps) or else a nonempty sequence of detailed steps for the gap, such as:

   = g(h(a,a,f4(f1(c))),g2(a,c))          by E2         
   = g(g1(f2(f4(a)),f4(e)),g2(a,c))       by E3         
   = g(g1(g(f3(a),f2(e)),f4(e)),g2(a,c))  by E4         

With this sequence of steps filling the gap, the original proof sketch is transformed into the following completely 
detailed proof: 

s = g(h(c,a,c),h(a,c,a))                                
  = g(h(a,a,f4(f1(c))),h(a,c,a))          by E1         
  = g(h(a,a,f4(f1(c))),g2(a,c))           by E2         
  = g(g1(f2(f4(a)),f4(e)),g2(a,c))        by E3         
  = g(g1(g(f3(a),f2(e)),f4(e)),g2(a,c))   by E4         
  = g(g1(g4(f1(f5(f3(a))),d),e),g2(a,c))  by E5, E6     

The goal of the input proof sketch is to rewrite a starting term to an ending term with a chain of equational-inference steps, 
each of which must be justified by appeal to certain equations that are taken for granted (as axioms). In particular, a fully 
detailed proof (without any gaps) consists of the following:

(1) a set EQN comprising m > 0 equational axioms E_1,...,E_m, m > 0; and 

(2) a sequence of n > 0 steps S_0, S_1, ..., S_n, where each step S_i consists of a term t_i, which we write as term(S_i), and a set
of equations drawn from EQN, which we write as EQN(S_i). We have EQN(S_0) = {}. The term of the first step S_0 is the starting term, 
the term of the last step S_n is the ending term, and the first step (S_0) does not have any equations (EQN(S_0) = {}). 
For every step S_i with i > 0, the equations EQN(S_i) are supposed to license the passage from term(S_{i-1}) to term(S_i). 
That is, the equations cited by step S_i, i > 0, *prove* that the term of that step can be derived from the term of the preceding step 
by simple equational reasoning. (Exactly what is meant by "simple equational reasoning" will be clarified shortly.)
Thus, the entire proof can be schematically understood as follows:

    term(S_0)
  = term(S_1)  by EQN(S_1)
  = term(S_2)  by EQN(S_2)
  ... 
  = term(S_n)  by EQN(S_n)

Note that EQN(S_i) should have at most 2 equations. You can't list 3 or more justifying equations for a step. 

Here is a concrete example of a full proof, with g(a,f(b)) as the starting term, g(f(f(f(b))),a) as the ending term, and 
EQN containing the two equations E1: f(X) = f(f(X)) and E2: g(X,Y) = g(Y,X). Variable names start with upper-case letters, 
whereas the names of function symbols and constants start with lower-case letters. Every equation is understood to be 
universally quantified over all the variables that occur in it. Thus, for example, E2 should be understood as 
(forall X, Y . g(X,Y) = g(Y,X)). The proof has 4 steps: 

s = g(a,f(b))                 # Step S_0
  = g(f(b),a)         by E2   # Step S_1
  = g(f(f(b)),a)      by E1   # Step S_2
  = g(f(f(f(b))),a)   by E1   # Step S_3 

This proof is correct, because the term of each step S_i for i > 0 can be derived from the previous term on the basis
of the cited equations (again, the exact definition of derivability is given below). In particular: 

Step S_1: By E2, we have g(X,Y) = g(Y,X) for all X, Y. Hence, the previous term, term(S_0) = g(a,f(b)), can be rewritten as 
term(S_1) = g(f(b),a) by switching the order of the two arguments. 

Step S_2: By E1, we have f(X) = f(f(X)) for all X, and hence f(b) = f(f(b)). Thus, term(S_1) = g(f(b),a) can be rewritten 
into g(f(f(b)),a), which is precisely term(S_2). 

Step S_3: Again by E1, we have f(X) = f(f(X)) for all X, and thus f(f(b)) = f(f(f(b))). Accordingly, term(S_2) = g(f(f(b)),a) 
can be rewritten into g(f(f(f(b))),a), which is term(S_3). This step concludes the proof successfully. 

In the gap-filling case, if you are given as input the proof sketch 

s = g(a,f(b))                
  = ??                ?? 
  = g(f(f(f(b))),a)   by E1  

you should be able to fill in the gap (designated by the question marks) with the two-step sequence: 

  = g(f(b),a)         by E2  
  = g(f(f(b)),a)      by E1  

thereby recovering the complete (concrete) original proof. 

In particular, your output will be a JSON dictionary with only 3 keys, "explanation", "missingSteps", and "confidence".

{"explanation": <An explanation, in English, of your reasoning strategy for solving this problem.>,

 "missingSteps": <A list of missing steps [step_1, ..., step_n], each of which is a JSON dictionary with 2 keys: 
                  {"term": <the term of this step>, 
                   "supportingEquations": <A list [...] of equation names that justify this step.>}>,

 "confidence": <An integer from 1 to 5, with 5 representing perfect confidence, 1 representing no confidence, 
                2 representing little confidence, 3 representing some (medium) confidence, and 4 representing
                strong but not quite perfect confidence.>}

You can indicate that that there is no valid way to fill in the missing gap by specifying the empty list [] 
as the value of "missingSteps". 

Note that your supportingEquations list for every step should not include any redundant elements. It should
include an equation if and only if that equation is actually needed for that particular step. 

We use the following alphabet of function symbols: 

Constant symbols            (arity 0): a, b, c, d, e         
Unary function symbols      (arity 1): f, f1, f2, f3, f4, f5
Binary function symbols     (arity 2): g, g1, g2, g3, g4, g5
Ternary function symbols    (arity 3): h, h1, h2, h3, h4, h5
Quaternary function symbols (arity 4): r, r1, r2, r3, r4, r5 

Constant symbols are not necessarily distinct. A certain set of equations might imply, for example,
the identity (a = b). 

Variable names start with upper-case letters: X, Y, V123, etc. 

Terms are essentially tree structures. Constant symbols and variables can be viewed as leaves, whereas 
a complex term like g(s,t) is a tree of height greater than 1, with the function symbol g at the root 
and the immediate subterms s and t as its subtrees. The notion of a subterm then corresponds to the notion 
of a subtree. For instance, the term t = h(a,f(b),g(c,d)) has the proper subterms a, f(b), b, g(c,d), c, and d. 
Every term is a subterm of itself, though not a proper subterm. Using Dewey-style positional notation to 
navigate trees, we may say that the root of the term t above, namely the symbol h, occurs at position []. 
At position [1] we find the constant symbol a. At position [2] we find the subterm f(b), and thus at 
position [2, 1] we find the constant symbol b. At position [3] we have g(c,d) and thus at positions [3,1] 
and [3,2] we have the constants c and d respectively. We can also associate these positions with the entire 
subterms rooted there, writing subterm(t,p) to denote the subterm of t found at position p:

subterm(t,[]) = h(a,f(b),g(c,d))
subterm(t,[1]) = a
subterm(t,[2]) = f(b)
subterm(t,[2,1]) = b 
subterm(t,[3]) = g(c,d)
subterm(t,[3,1]) = c
subterm(t,[3,2]) = d 

Note that subterm is a partial function, meaning that subterm(t,p) is undefined if p is not a 
valid position in the tree representation of t. 

Two positions are disjoint iff neither is a prefix of the other. 

A substitution theta is a finite function from variables to terms, e.g., theta = {V12 = a, V354 = f(b)}. 
To *apply* such a substitution theta to a term t is simply to replace every variable v in t that occurs 
in the domain of theta with the corresponding term, theta(v). For instance, applying the above substitution 
to the term s = h(a,V12, g(V12,V354)) produces the term theta(s) = h(a,a,g(a,f(b))). We say that 
a term t *matches* a term s (or is "an instance" of s) iff there is a substitution theta such that 
theta(s) = t. For example, g(X,f(b)) matches g(X,Y) under the substitution {Y = f(b)}. 

If e is an equation (s1 = s2), we write LHS(e) and RHS(e) for s1 and s2, respectively. And we say that a term s 
*reduces* or *rewrites* to a term t via e, written as s -->_e t, iff there is a position p such that subterm(s,p) 
matches LHS(e) under some substitution theta and t is identical to s except that subterm(t,p) = theta(RHS(e)). 
We refer to subterm(s,p) as a *redex* of s under e, or as an "e-redex", and we refer to subterm(t,p) as the 
corresponding *contractum*. Thus, t is obtained from s by replacing the e-redex at position p by the corresponding 
contractum. 

If E = {e_1,...,e_k} is a set of k > 0 equations, we write s ==>_E t iff s contains k distinct redexes,
one for each e_i in E, in k *disjoint* positions; and t is obtained from s by replacing each of these 
k redexes by the corresponding contractum. Thus, if s ==>_E t then we have 
s -->_{e_1} s_1 -->_{e_2} s_2 ... -->_{e_k} s_k = t for k-1 intermediate terms s_i. However, because
the k redexes occur in disjoint positions of s, we can imagine all of these reductions occurring concurrently
in one fell swoop. Note that, by definition, if s ==>_E t then E is minimal in the sense that it has no 
redundant equations; all k equations in E are used to reduce k redexes in s. We write <==>_E for the symmetric
closure of ==>_E, so that s <==>_E t iff either s ==>_E t or t ==>_E s. 

We can now specify exactly what it means to say that a term t can be derived from a term s 
by "simple equational reasoning". It means that there is a subset E of EQN, having exactly k > 0 equations, 
such that s <==>_E t. Thus, the "supportingEquations" you list for a given step of the proof must comprise a set
E such that s <==>_E t, where t is the step's term and s is the previous step's term. 

For instance, consider again the proof example above:

s = g(a,f(b))                 # Step S_0
  = g(f(b),a)         by E2   # Step S_1
  = g(f(f(b)),a)      by E1   # Step S_2
  = g(f(f(f(b))),a)   by E1   # Step S_3 

where E1 is f(X) = f(f(X) and E2 is g(X,Y) = g(Y,X). Looking at step S_1, we see that the entire preceding term, term(S_0), 
namely g(a,f(b)), is a redex, matching the LHS of E2 under the substitution {X = a, Y = f(b)}. Applying this substitution 
at the matching position, [], means replacing that subterm with the corresponding instance of the right-hand side of E2, 
g(Y,X), i.e., with the contractum g(f(b),a), thus g(a,f(b)) ==>_{E2} g(f(b),a). 

Likewise, looking at step S_2, in going from term(S_1) = g(f(b),a) to term(S_2) = g(f(f(b)),a) by virtue of E1, we see that
the subterm f(b) of term(S_1), at position [1], matches the LHS of E1 under theta = {X = b}. Replacing that redex 
with the corresponding instance of the RHS of E1, namely the contractum f(f(b)), produces term(S_2) = g(f(f(b)),a),
and thus term(S_1) ==>_{E1} term(S_2). Repeating this process one more time finally takes us from S_2 to S_3, which concludes the proof. 

It follows that if you are going from a term s to a term t and citing two supporting equations, either s must have two subterms 
in disjoint positions, matching the left-hand sides of these two equations, and t must be obtained by reducing these two redexes,
or conversely, t must have two such redexes and s must be obtained by reducing those two redexes. Remember that you cannot cite
more than 2 equations per step. And it follows that you should set the value of 'missingSteps' to be the empty list iff the gap
cannot be closed, i.e., iff there is no sequence of steps connecting the term right before the beginning of the gap to the term
right after the gap where each step adheres to the requirements listed above.
