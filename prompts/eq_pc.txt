Your objective is to check whether an equational proof is correct, and to justify your answer. The proof is given as 
a chain of steps that rewrite a starting term to an ending term. Each step is justified by appeal to certain equations
that are taken for granted. In particular, your input will consist of the following:

(1) a set EQN comprising m > 0 equations E_1,...,E_m, representing the available equational axioms; and 

(2) a sequence of n > 0 steps S_0, S_1, ..., S_n, where each step S_i consists of a term t_i, which we write as term(S_i), 
and a set of equations drawn from EQN, which we write as EQN(S_i). We have EQN(S_0) = {}. The term of the first step S_0 
is the starting term, the term of the last step S_n is the ending term, and the first step (S_0) does not have any equations. 
For every step S_i with i > 0, the equations EQN(S_i) are supposed to license the passage from term(S_{i-1}) to term(S_i). 
That is, the equations cited by step S_i, i > 0, prove that the term of that step can be derived from the term of the 
preceding step by simple equational reasoning (exactly what constitutes "simple equational reasoning" will be specified
in detail shortly). Thus, the proof can be schematically understood as follows:

    term(S_0)
  = term(S_1)  by EQN(S_1)
  = term(S_2)  by EQN(S_2)
  ... 
  = term(S_n)  by EQN(S_n)

Your output will be a JSON dictionary with two keys: 

{"correct": <true or false, according to whether or not the input proof is correct>,
 "explanation":   <If the proof is correct, the value of this key should be a list of n dictionaries, one for each 
                   step S_i with i > 0, showing exactly how that step derives its term from the term of the previous 
		   step. (The exact format is precisely specified below and illustrated with examples.) If the proof 
		   is incorrect, the value of this key should be a dictionary pinpointing the first offending step 
		   in the proof, as well as the details of the error. See below for a precise specification.>}

Here is an example, with g(a,f(b)) as the starting term, g(f(f(f(b))),a) as the ending term, and EQN containing the 
two equations E1: f(X) = f(f(X)) and E2: g(X,Y) = g(Y,X). Variable names start with upper-case letters, whereas the names
of function symbols and constants start with lower-case letters. Every equation is understood to be universally quantified
over all the variables that occur in it. Thus, for example, E2 should be understood as (forall X, Y . g(X,Y) = g(Y,X)). 
The proof has 4 steps: 

s = g(a,f(b))                 # Step S_0
  = g(f(b),a)         by E2   # Step S_1
  = g(f(f(b)),a)      by E1   # Step S_2
  = g(f(f(f(b))),a)   by E1   # Step S_3 

This proof is correct, because the term of each step S_i for i > 0 can be derived from the previous term on the basis
of the cited equations. In other words, the identity term(S_{i-1}) = term(S_i) follows logically from the given equations.
In particular: 

Step S_1: By E2, we have g(X,Y) = g(Y,X) for all X, Y. Hence, the previous term, term(S_0) = g(a,f(b)), 
can be rewritten as term(S_1) = g(f(b),a) by switching the order of the two arguments. 

Step S_2: By E1, we have f(X) = f(f(X)) for all X, and hence f(b) = f(f(b)). Thus, term(S_1) = g(f(b),a) 
can be rewritten into g(f(f(b)),a), which is precisely term(S_2). 

Step S_3: Again by E1, we have f(X) = f(f(X)) for all X, and thus f(f(b)) = f(f(f(b))). Accordingly, 
term(S_2) = g(f(f(b)),a) can be rewritten into g(f(f(f(b))),a), which is term(S_3). This step concludes 
the proof successfully. 

We use the following alphabet of function symbols: 

Constant symbols            (arity 0): a, b, c, d, e         
Unary function symbols      (arity 1): f, f1, f2, f3, f4, f5
Binary function symbols     (arity 2): g, g1, g2, g3, g4, g5
Ternary function symbols    (arity 3): h, h1, h2, h3, h4, h5
Quaternary function symbols (arity 4): r, r1, r2, r3, r4, r5 

Note that the constant symbols are not necessarily distinct. A certain set of equations might imply, 
for example, the identity (a = b). 

Variable names start with upper-case letters: X, Y, V123, etc. 

Terms are essentially tree structures. Constant symbols and variables can be viewed as leaves, whereas 
a complex term like g(s,t) is a tree of height greater than 1, with the function symbol g at the root 
and the immediate subterms s and t as its subtrees, from left to right. The notion of a subterm then 
corresponds to the notion of a subtree. For instance, the term t = h(a,f(b),g(c,d)) has the proper subterms 
a, f(b), b, g(c,d), c, and d. Every term is a subterm of itself, though not a proper subterm. 
Using Dewey-style positional notation to navigate trees, we may say that the root of the term t above, 
namely the symbol h, occurs at position []. At position [1] we find the constant symbol a. At position [2] 
we find the subterm f(b), and thus at position [2, 1] we find the constant symbol b. At position [3] 
we have g(c,d) and thus at positions [3,1] and [3,2] we have the constants c and d respectively. 
We can also associate these positions with the entire subterms rooted there, writing subterm(t,p) 
to denote the subterm of t at position p:

subterm(t,[]) = h(a,f(b),g(c,d))
subterm(t,[1]) = a
subterm(t,[2]) = f(b)
subterm(t,[2,1]) = b 
subterm(t,[3]) = g(c,d)
subterm(t,[3,1]) = c
subterm(t,[3,2]) = d 

Note that subterm is a partial function, meaning that subterm(t,p) is undefined if p is not a 
valid position in the tree representation of t. 

Two positions are disjoint iff neither is a prefix of the other. 

A substitution theta is a finite function from variables to terms, e.g., theta = {V12 = a, V354 = f(b)}. 
To *apply* such a substitution theta to a term t is simply to replace every variable v in t that occurs 
in the domain of theta with the corresponding term, theta(v). For instance, applying the above substitution 
to the term s = h(a,V12, g(V12,V354)) produces the term theta(s) = h(a,a,g(a,f(b))). We say that 
a term t *matches* a term s (or is "an instance" of s) iff there is a substitution theta such that 
theta(s) = t. For example, g(X,f(b)) matches g(X,Y) under the substitution {Y = f(b)}. 

If e is an equation (s1 = s2), we write LHS(e) and RHS(e) for s1 and s2, respectively. And we say that a term s 
*reduces* or *rewrites* to a term t via e, written as s -->_e t, iff there is a position p such that subterm(s,p) 
matches LHS(e) under some substitution theta and t is identical to s except that subterm(t,p) = theta(RHS(e)). 
We refer to subterm(s,p) as a *redex* of s under e, or as an "e-redex", and we refer to subterm(t,p) as the 
corresponding *contractum*. Thus, t is obtained from s by replacing the e-redex at position p by the corresponding 
contractum. 

If E = {e_1,...,e_k} is a set of k > 0 equations, we write s ==>_E t iff s contains k distinct redexes,
one for each e_i in E, in k *disjoint* positions; and t is obtained from s by replacing each of these 
k redexes by the corresponding contractum. Thus, if s ==>_E t then we have 
s -->_{e_1} s_1 -->_{e_2} s_2 ... -->_{e_k} s_k = t for k-1 intermediate terms s_i. However, because
the k redexes occur in disjoint positions of s, we can imagine all of these reductions occurring concurrently
in one fell swoop. Note that, by definition, if s ==>_E t then E is minimal in the sense that it has no 
redundant equations; all k equations in E are used to reduce k redexes in s. 

We can now specify exactly what it means to say that a term t can be derived from a term s 
on the basis of k > 0 given equations E = {E_1, ..., E_k}: it simply means that s ==>_E t. 
For instance, consider again the proof example above:

s = g(a,f(b))                 # Step S_0
  = g(f(b),a)         by E2   # Step S_1
  = g(f(f(b)),a)      by E1   # Step S_2
  = g(f(f(f(b))),a)   by E1   # Step S_3 

where E1 is f(X) = f(f(X) and E2 is g(X,Y) = g(Y,X). Looking at step S_1, we see that the entire preceding 
term, term(S_0), namely g(a,f(b)), is a redex, matching the LHS of E2 under the substitution {X = a, Y = f(b)}. 
Applying this substitution at the matching position, [], means replacing that subterm with the corresponding 
instance of the right-hand side of E2, g(Y,X), i.e., with the contractum g(f(b),a). 

Likewise, looking at step S_2, in going from term(S_1) = g(f(b),a) to term(S_2) = g(f(f(b)),a) by virtue of E1, 
we see that the subterm f(b) of term(S_1), at position [1], matches the LHS of E1 under theta = {X = b}. 
Replacing that redex with the corresponding instance of the RHS of E1, namely the contractum f(f(b)), 
produces term(S_2) = g(f(f(b)),a). Repeating this process one more time finally takes us from S_2 to S_3, 
which concludes the proof. 

Note that in going from a term s to a term t, there may be multiple subterms of s (in disjoint positions) 
matching the left-hand sides of multiple equations. For instance, we can validly go from s = h(a,g(b,c),f(d)) 
to t = h(a,g(c,b),f(f(d))) by virtue of E1 and E2 because: the subterm g(b,c) at position [2] is a redex that 
matches the LHS of E2 under {X = b, Y = c}, while the subterm f(d) at position [3] is another redex that matches 
the LHS of E1 under X = d. Thus, we can replace the former redex at [2] by the corresponding instance of E2's RHS, 
namely, the contractum g(c,b), and the latter subterm at [3] by the corresponding instance of E1's RHS, namely 
the contractum f(f(d)). Doing so yields the term t = h(a,g(c,b),f(f(d))). In the general case of going 
from s to t, there may be more than 2 redexes of s, matching multiple equations, whose replacements produce t.

To sum up, your output must be a JSON dictionary with two keys: "correct", whose value will be true or false
according to whether or not the given proof is correct, and "explanation", which shows why the proof is correct
or incorrect. In particular, for a correct proof with n+1 steps, the value of the "explanation" key should be 
a list of n dictionaries of this form:

{"step": ..., "rewrites": [..., {"position": ..., "redex": "...", "equation": ..., "contractum": "..."}, ...]} 

For an incorrect proof, the value of the "explanation" key should be either of the form 

{"step": ..., "equation": ..., "position": ..., "actualContractum": ..., "expectedContractum": ...}, 

indicating a wrongly applied matching substitution, or of the form 

{"step": ..., "givenEquations": [...], "correctEquations:[...]},

indicating that the cited equations do not justify the passage to the current term. If there is a set of 
equations E that justifies rewriting the previous term to this step's term, in the sense of ==>_E, then 
those equation names should be listed as the value of "correctEquations". If there are no equations that 
can license the passage to the current term according to ==>_E, then the value of "correctEquations" 
should be the empty list.
