You will be given as input a formal proof for a propositional-logic problem where certain parts
of the proof have been removed, leaving gaps of various sizes in their places. Your objective is 
to fill those gaps with appropriate subproofs. 

The problem that the proof solves consists of a number of formulas p_1, ..., p_n that are taken 
as premises (where n >= 0) and a goal p, which is a single target formula representing the 
desired conclusion. A formula p is either an atom like A, B, C, D, E, ..., as well as the 
constants true and false, or else: a negation, of the form (~ p); a conjunction, of the form 
(p1 & p2); a disjunction, of the form (p1 | p2); a conditional (p1 ==> p2); or a biconditional 
(p1 <==> p2). We say that two formulas are "complementary" (or "complements", or that they 
"complement" each other) when one is the syntactic negation of the other. This is a symmetric 
relation: p complements q iff q complements p. 

Precedence and associativity are as follows: Negation binds the tightest, followed 
by conjunction, disjunction, conditionals and biconditionals. All binary logical constructors 
associate to the right. Thus, for instance, (~A & B ==> C | D) should be understood as 
(((~ A) & B) ==> (C | D)), and these two formulas should be considered identical, and the same 
as (~A & B ==> (C | D)), or ((~A & B) ==> C | D), or ((~A) & B ==> (C | D)), and so on; 
all of these are identical, given the precedence and associativity conventions just specified. 

If a formula is complex (not atomic), then it must be enclosed within parentheses. Thus,
for example, writing ~A or writing B ==> C is not valid syntax; these formulas should instead 
be written as (~ A) and (B ==> C), respectively. However, within the outer pair of parentheses,  
additional parentheses may be (optionally) skipped according to the specified precedence 
and associativity conventions, e.g., it's fine to write (~A & ~B ==> ~B & ~A) instead of 
(((~ A) & (~ B)) ==> ((~ B) & (~ A))). 

The goal p is definitely derivable from the premises p_1, ..., p_n, and the gaps can definitely
be filled in some way or another, so as to yield a fully detailed proof that derives p from the
premises. 

Think about the problem carefully in a step-by-step way and only write down your gap
assignments at the end. Your answer should be in the following form:

GAP-1:
<the right proof for GAP-1>
GAP-2:
<the right proof for GAP-2>
...

Essentially, after replacing every gap with the specified subproof, the fully detailed
proof that results must correctly derive the goal from the premises. 

The input proof (and every subproof you will write for a gap) is written in NDL, short for 
"Natural Deduction Language". In particular, an NDL proof is either an application of single 
inference rule to a number of formulas; or a composite proof, built by stringing together a number 
of other proofs; or a conditional (hypothetical) proof. Applications of inference rules are 
primitive proofs with no internal structure, while composite and hypothetical proofs are compound, 
having recursive structure. More specifically: 

1. A primitive proof is a single-line application of an inference rule R to a number of formulas 
p_1,...,p_n,  each of which is either an asserted premise, or a currently active assumption, 
or else was derived by a previous subproof. Such a single-step proof is of the form 
"R on p_1,...,p_n", where R is the name of the inference rule and p_1, ..., p_n are its arguments; 
or, alternatively, "p BY R on p_1,...,p_n", where p is the conclusion obtained by the application 
of R to p_1,...,p_n. For instance, an application of mp (modus ponens) might be written as either: 

mp on (A ==> C), A

or as:

C BY mp on (A ==> C), A 

The use of the BY keyword emphasizes that the conclusion of the step is C, which is obtained 
BY applying the rule mp (modus ponens) to two arguments: the conditional (A ==> C) and the atom A. 

In addition, a rule application (like other subproofs in NDL) can optionally be given an arbitrary 
name (identifier) I, so its full syntax takes one of 4 forms: either 

* R on p_1,...,p_n (unnamed version, no BY); or 

* I := R on p_1,...,p_n (named version, no BY); or 

* p BY R on p_1,...,p_n (unnamed version, with BY); or finally:

* I := p BY R on p_1,...,p_n (named  version, with BY).

With naming, subsequent steps in the proof can use the name I to refer to the formula 
that is produced by this rule application (but only inside the same block where it was 
introduced; see the information below concerning scope and proof blocks). 

When "p BY ..." is used, the step ... to the right of BY cannot produce an arbitrary 
conclusion q -- it *must* specifically produce p. 

2. Multiple proofs D_1, ..., D_n, D_{n+1} can be combined into a single *proof block* 
by enclosing them within curly braces and separating them with semicolons:

{
  D_1;
  ...
  D_n;
  D_{n+1} 
} 

Note the indentation (by two blanks). Also note that there is no semicolon after the last element 
(D_{n+1}), although one could optionally appear there. In other words, semicolons are mandatory 
only in between consecutive steps, but may or may not appear after the trailing step (i.e., 
immediately before a closing brace, "}"). 

Composition is how lemma formation works in NDL: The conclusion of each D_i becomes logically 
available to every subsequent D_j for j > i, i.e., every such D_j can use the conclusion of D_i 
as if it were a premise. 

Consider, for instance, the two-step composition
{ 
  (~ B) by mp on (A ==> ~ B), A;
  (C & ~ B) BY both on C, (~ B)
}

Here, (~ B) is derived by the fist step, the application of mp, and hence it can be freely 
used as an argument to the subsequent application of both. 

Note the following important point: The conclusion produced by the entire composition 
{D_1; ...; D_n; D_{n+1}} is the conclusion of the very last element of the composition, 
i.e., the conclusion of the proof D_{n+1}. In the above example, the conclusion produced 
by the two-element composition 

{ 
  (~ B) by mp on (A ==> ~ B), A;
  (C & ~ B) BY both on C, (~ B)
}

is the conclusion produced by the second subproof proof, namely (C & (~ B)). 

Any component subproof D_j inside a proof block D of the form {D_1; D_2; ...; D_n; D_{n+1}}
can be given a name, by writing D_j as "I_j := D_j". This is so even if D_j is not a primitive
proof (i.e., a rule application). But note that such a name binding will only be visible to 
the subsequent subproofs D_{j+1}, ..., D_n, D_{n+1} inside the block D. I_j will no longer 
denote the conclusion of the subproof D_j once control moves past the proof block D. 

Note also that an intermediate subproof D_j with j <= n may be a simple abbreviation rather 
than an actual proof, i.e., essentially D_j can be a definition of the form "I := p". 
Such a step simply serves to give the name I to the formula p during the subsequent
subproofs D_{j+1}, ..., D_n, D_{n+1} inside D. Note that j <= n implies that j =/= n+1,
meaning that D_j cannot be the trailing (very last) component D_{n+1}. Such a purely
definitional step can only appear before the end of a composition. 

3. Below is the list of inference rules that can be used with primitive rule applications in NDL. 
Each can only be applied to a specific number of formulas, and oftentimes those formulas need to 
have a specific syntactic form, otherwise the application results in an error. 

  Rule 1: Name: claim (reiteration)  
          Description: Applied to a formula p that holds, it returns p again as the conclusion. 

  Rule 2: Name: left-and (conjunction elimination)
          Description: Applied to a conjunction (p & q) that holds, it detaches the left component p. 

  Rule 3: Name: right-and (conjunction elimination) 
          Description: Applied to a conjunction (p & q) that holds, it detaches the right component q.

  Rule 4: Name: both (conjunction introduction)
          Description: Applied to two formulas p and q that hold, it produces the conjunction (p & q). 

  Rule 5: Name: mp (modus ponens, conditional elimination) 
          Description: Applied to two formulas (p ==> q) and p that hold, it produces the conclusion q. 

  Rule 6: Name: left-either (disjunction introduction)
          Description: Applied to two formulas p and q, the left of which (p) holds, it produces the 
	  disjunction (p | q). The right formula can be any formula whatsoever, and might or might not hold. 	  

  Rule 7: Name: right-either (disjunction introduction)
          Description: Applied to two formulas p and q, the right of which (q) holds, it produces the 
	  disjunction (p | q). The left formula can be any formula whatsoever, and might or might not hold. 	  

  Rule 8: Name: cases (disjunction elimination)
          Description: Applied to three formulas of the form (p | q), (p ==> r) and (q ==> r) that hold, 
	  it produces the conclusion r. 

  Rule 9: Name: absurd (false introduction):
          Description: Applied to two complementary formulas of the form p and (~ p) (or (~ p) and p), 
	  both of which hold, it produces the conclusion false. 
          
  Rule 10: Name: dn (negation elimination, aka "double negation"):
           Description: Applied to a formula of the form (~ ~ p) that holds, it produces the conclusion p. 

  Rule 11: Name: left-iff (biconditional elimination)
           Description: Applied to a formula of the form (p <==> q) that holds, it produces the 
	   conclusion (p ==> q). 

  Rule 12: Name: right-iff (biconditional elimination)
           Description: Applied to a formula of the form (p <==> q) that holds, it produces 
	   the conclusion (q ==> p). 

  Rule 13: Name: equiv (biconditional introduction) 
           Description: Applied to two formulas of the form (p ==> q) and (q ==> p) that hold, it produces 
	   the conclusion (p <==> q). 

  Rule 14: Name: ex-middle (excluded middle)
           Description: Applied to any formula p, regardless of whether p holds, it produces 
	   the conclusion (p | ~p). 

  Rule 15: Name: dm (De Morgan)
           Description: dm can be applied to any formula of the form (~ (p & q)) that holds, producing 
	   the conclusion (~p | ~q). It can also be applied to a formula of the form (~ (p | q)) 
	   that hods, producing the conclusion (~p & ~q).

  Rule 16: Name: dsyl (disjunctive syllogism):
           Description: dsyl is applied to two arguments of the form (p | q) and r, where r complements 
	   either p or q (meaning that either r and p are complements or else r and q are complements). 
	   Both of these arguments must hold. If r complements p, the result is q; and if r complements q,
	   the result is p. For instance, if (A | B) and (~ B) hold, then the application 
	   "dsyl on (A | B), (~ B)" will produce the conclusion A. Likewise, if (A | B) and (~ A) both 
	   hold, then applying dsyl to (~A | C), A will produce C. 

  Rule 17: Name: neg-cond (negated conditional) 
           Description: If a negated conditional of the form (~ (p ==> q)) holds, then applying neg-cond 
	   to it will produce the conjunction (p & (~ q)). This reflects the definition of a conditional 
	   (p ==> q), according to which (p ==> q) is false if and only if p holds and q doesn't. 
           Conversely, if (p & ~ q) holds, then applying neg-cond to it will derive (~ (p ==> q)). 

  Rule 18: Name: neg-bicond (negated biconditional): 
           Description: If a negated biconditional of the form (~ (p <==> q)) holds, then applying 
	   neg-bicond to it will produce the disjunction ((p & (~ q)) | ((~ p) & q)). This reflects 
	   the definition of a biconditional (p <==> q), which says that (p <==> q) holds if and only if 
	   p and q both hold or else neither one does. Thus, if (p <==> q) fails, this means that either 
	   p holds and q doesn't, or q does and p doesn't. Conversely, if the disjunction 
	   ((p & (~ q)) | ((~ p) & q)) holds, applying neg-bicond to it will derive (~ (p <==> q)). 

  Rule 19: Name: from-false (introduction of any formula from a contradiction):
           Description: This is a unary inference rule that takes an arbitrary formula p as input. 
	   The result is p, *provided* that the constant false currently holds (this is a very important 
	   precondition). It is an error is false doesn't hold.

  Rule 20: Name: mt (modus tollens)
           Description: Suppose that two formulas of the form (p ==> q) and r hold, where r complements q. 
	   Then applying mt to these two formulas will produce the conclusion (~ p). 

  Rule 21: Name: cond-def (conditional definition)
           Description: Suppose that a conditional (p ==> q) holds. Then applying cond-def to it will 
	   produce the conclusion (~ p | q). Conversely, if (~ p | q) holds, then applying cond-def to 
	   it will produce the conclusion (p ==> q). 

  Rule 22: Name: bicond-def' (biconditional definition)
           Description: Suppose that a biconditional (p <==> q) holds. Then applying bicond-def' to it 
	   will produce the disjunction ((p & q) |(~ p & ~ q)), which states that either both p and q hold 
	   or neither does. That is an alternative definition of biconditionals. Conversely, 
	   if a formula of the form ((p & q) | ((~ p) & (~ q))) holds, then applying bicond-def' to it
           will produce the biconditional (p <==> q). 

  Rule 23: Name: from-complements (infer anything from two complementary formulas)
           Description: Suppose that a formula q holds and that a complementary formula r also holds
	   (so that q and r complement each other). Then applying the ternary rule from-complements 
	   to any formulas p, q, and r will produce the conclusion p. So the first argument is the
	   conclusion we want to derive and the last two arguments are the two complementary formulas
	   that hold. This is valid because anything follows from an inconsistency. Note that 
	   from-complements is not strictly necessary, since the same result can be obtained by first 
	   applying absurd to q and (~ q) to derive false, and then applying from-false to p would
           successfully derive p. But from-complements is more convenient because it captures both 
	   steps in a single rule application. 

  Rule 24: Name: by-contradiction (reasoning by contradiction).
           Description: This binary rule is applied to formulas of the form p and (q ==> false), 
	   where q complements p. Assuming that the second argument (q ==> false) holds, the first 
	   argument p is produced as the conclusion. This is how reasoning by contradiction is performed
 	   in this language: If one wants to derive p by way of contradiction, they first prove 
	   a conditional of the form (q ==> false), where q and p are complementary, then apply 
	   the rule by-contradiction to p and (q ==> false). NDL provides another way to perform 
	   reasoning by contradiction, using the suppose-absurd construct described below. 
	   But combinations of assume followed by applications of by-contradiction are the most 
	   common method. We stress that the first argument to this rule, p, does NOT need to hold 
	   at the time when this rule is applied; only the second argument, the conditional (q ==> false), 
	   needs to hold. The first argument p is essentially just an annotation: it simply indicates 
	   the desired conclusion of the entire application of by-contradiction; if the rule application 
	   works, it will return the first argument p as the result. 

By the way, when we say that a formula "holds" in NDL, we mean that it is either a starting premise 
that was initially asserted; or it is an active hypothesis postulated by a currently open conditional 
('assume') subproof; or else it was derived at a previous point in the proof (by a previous element 
of a proof composition) and remains operative in the current scope. It's always an error if an 
inference rule expects an argument to hold and it doesn't. 

4. Conditional (or hypothetical) proofs in NDL are compound proofs of the form 

assume p {
  ...  
}

where p is the formula being assumed (the hypothesis) and { ... } is a subproof comprising 
the *body* of the conditional proof, which represents the logical *scope* of the hypothesis p. 
Accordingly, the hypothesis p remains operative --as a working assumption -- throughout the 
body { ... } and can be freely used as an argument to any inference rule inside that body, 
as if it were a starting premise. 

If the body produces a conclusion q, then the entire 'assume p { ... }' proof discharges 
the assumption p and ultimately produces the conditional conclusion (p ==> q). 
The hypothesis p is no longer active after that point. 
  
Thus, for instance: 

assume A { ...
  ... 
  # Here we are inside the scope of A 
  assume B { 
    ... 
    # Here we are inside the scope of both A and B
  }; 
  ... 
  # Here we are again only inside the scope of A 
}; 
... # And here we are outside the scope of both A and B 

Optionally, a name I can be given to the hypothesis of a conditional proof, e.g., one may write 

assume I := p { ... }
 
instead of simply 

assume p { ... }

In the former version, the name I can be used to refer to the formula p anywhere inside the body
of the conditional proof, { ... }. But this binding of I to p does not remain active outside of
that body. 

5. Reductio ad absurdum proofs (proofs by contradiction) are similar to conditional proofs. 
They are of the form: 

suppose-absurd p {
  ...
}

where p is the formula we are trying to disprove. In other words, we are trying to prove the negation of p, (~ p). 
If, with the aid of the assumption p, the body ... manages to produce the conclusion false, then the entire 
'suppose-absurd p { ... }' proof derives the conclusion (~ p). A suppose-absurd proof can be viewed as 
a slightly shorter version of a proof that uses assume followed by an application of by-contradiction. 

6. In NDL, a pound sign ("#") is used to write comments that extend to the end of the line. 
Your input NDL proof might or might not have comments.

7. Starting premises are written as follows:

assert <premise-name> := p 

where <premise-name> is an identifier (just an alphanumeric string starting with an alphabetic letter,
typically a name like 'premise-4' or 'premise-12') and p is any formula. Such a directive serves as 
a global declaration to the effect that p is a premise that a proof can take for granted, and also 
serves to establish the identifier <premise-name> as a name for that premise, so that <premise-name> 
can then be used inside a proof to refer to the premise p. But note that p can also appear inline 
inside the proof (e.g., as a direct argument to an inference rule), so the use of <premise-name> 
as a shorthand for p is optional, not mandatory.
