You will be asked to write a Fitch-style natural-deduction proof for a propositional-logic problem 
that consists of a number of premises and a given conclusion. Your output will be a JSON dictionary 
with two keys: {"description": <English description of the proof>, "proof": <formal proof>}, 
where <formal proof> will be your proof and <English description of the proof> will be a high-level 
description of the strategy behind the proof. Your output must be only the above JSON dictionary, 
nothing else. 
                                                                                                          
A formula p here is either an atom like A, B, C, D, E, A1, B2, A3, E4, A5, A1_2, E3_59, etc., or else: 
a negation, of the form (~ p); a conjunction, of the form (p1 & p2); a disjunction, of the form (p1 | p2); 
a conditional (p1 ==> p2); or a biconditional (p1 <==> p2). Precedence and associativity are as follows: 
Negation binds the tightest, followed by conjunction, disjunction, conditionals and biconditionals, in
that order. All binary logical constructors associate to the right, so (A & B & C) is (A & (B & C)). 
Thus, (~A & B ==> C | D) should be understood as (((~ A) & B) ==> (C | D)), and these two formulas 
should be considered identical, given the precedence and associativity conventions just specified. 

Every complex (non-atomic) formula must be parenthesized, but inside complex formulas, parentheses 
around negations are optional. Thus, you can write (~A & ~B) as a shorthand for ((~ A) & (~ B)). 
But you may not write ~A, because that is a complex sentence (a negation) and thus it must have at least 
one outer pair of parentheses; it should instead be written as (~ A). While you should try to parenthesize 
fully in order to eliminate ambiguity, do not insert redundant/superfluous pairs of parentheses, e.g., 
do not write ((A & B)) or (((~A))). These should be written simply as (A & B) and (~ A). 

Your proof must be written in a formal language called NDL, which implements Fitch-style natural 
deduction. In this language, proofs have formal syntax and semantics.

An NDL proof is either an application of the inference rule 'from' to a number of formulas; 
or a composite proof, built by stringing together a number of other proofs; or a hypothetical 
(conditional) proof. Applications of the 'from' rule are primitive proofs with no internal structure, 
while composite and hypothetical proofs are compound, having recursive structure. 

More specifically: 

1. A primitive NDL proof is a single-line application of the form "p from p_1, ..., p_n".
We refer to the formulas p_i as the "justifiers" of this inference step. We must have n <= 5, 
i.e., there can never be more than 5 comma-separated justifiers. 

The objective of such a "from" step is to derive p from the justifiers p_1,...,p_n; 
if p does follow logically from p_1,...,p_n, then it becomes the *conclusion* of this step; 
but if p does not follow, then the step results in an error. So every time you write 
a "from" step, you must make absolutely sure that:
(a) the advertised conclusion p follows logically from the listed justifiers (i.e., 
that it's impossible for the justifiers to hold without the conclusion p holding as well); and
(b) n <= 5, i.e., there are no more that 5 justifiers. 

[When we say that p follows logically from p_1,...,p_n, we mean the classical definition of 
logical consequence: there is no interpretation that satisfies every p_i but falsifies p.]

Such a step can optionally be given an arbitrary name (identifier) I, by writing it as follows:
'I := p from p_1,...,p_n'. The name I can then be used to refer to the conclusion p later in
the proof (though only inside the same block where it was introduced; see the information below 
concerning scope). Thus, the full syntax of such a step is either "p from p_1, ..., p_n" OR 
"I := p from p_1, ..., p_n". 

Accordingly, a justifier p_i can be either: 

* a fully written out formula, e.g., (A ==> B); or 

* the name of a formula, e.g. lemma-34, where lemma-34 was introduced by a previous step 
of the form 'lemma-34 := p from p_1, ..., p_n" and remains active; or 

* the name of a premise, given by a initial declaration like 'assert premise-3 := ...'; 
or, finally: 

* the name of a hypothesis introduced by a conditional proof (see below), which also 
remains currently active. 

2. Multiple proofs D_1, ..., D_n, D_{n+1} can be combined into a single Fitch-style block 
by enclosing them within curly braces and separating them with semicolons:

{
  D_1;
  ...
  D_n;
  D_{n+1} 
} 

Note the indentation (by two blanks). Also note that a semicolon after the last element (D_{n+1}) 
is optional. 

Composition is how lemma formation works in NDL: The conclusion of each D_i becomes logically 
available to every subsequent D_j for j > i, i.e., every such D_j can use the conclusion of D_i 
as if it were a premise. 

Consider, for instance, the two-step composition
{ 
  (~ B) from (A ==> ~B), A;
  (C & ~B) from C, (~ B) 
}

Here, (~ B) is derived by the fist step and hence it can be freely used as a justifier 
for the following step. 

Note the following important point: The conclusion produced by the entire composition {D_1; ...; D_n; D_{n+1}} 
is the conclusion of the very last element of the composition, i.e., the conclusion of the proof D_{n+1}. 
In the above example,the conclusion produced by the two-element composition 

{ 
  (~ B) from (A ==> ~B), A;
  (C & ~B) from C, (~ B)
}

is the conclusion produced by the second proof step, namely (C & ~B). 

When we say that a formula "holds" in NDL, we mean that it is either a starting premise that was initially 
asserted; or it is an active hypothesis postulated by a currently open 'assume' subproof; or else it was derived 
at a previous point in the proof (by a previous subproof) and remains operative in the current scope. 

4. Conditional (or hypothetical) proofs in NDL are compound (recursive) proofs of the form 

assume p {
  ...  
}

where p is the formula being assumed (the hypothesis) and { ... } is a subproof comprising the *body* of the hypothetical proof
and the logical *scope* of the hypothesis p. That is, the hypothesis p remains operative (as a working assumption) anywhere 
inside the body { ... } and can be freely used as an argument to any inference rule inside that body, as if it were a starting premise. 
  
If the body produces a conclusion q, then the entire 'assume p { ... }' proof discharges the assumption p and 
finally produces the conditional conclusion (p ==> q). The hypothesis p is no longer active after that point. 
Thus, for instance: 

...
assume A { 
   ... 

   # Here we are inside the scope of A 

   assume B { 
     ... 

     # Here we are inside the scope of both A and B
     ... 
   }; 
  ... 
     # Here we are again only inside the scope of A 
}; 

... # And here we are outside the scope of both A and B 

A hypothesis may also be named, so a conditional proof may be of the form 

  assume I := p {
    ...  # Inside this scope, the identifier I can be used to refer to the formula p. 
  }
  # At this point we are outside the scope of the assume, so the identifier I no longer refers to p! 

Note that the name I has limited scope: it can ONLY be used to refer to the hypothesis p inside the scope of p, 
i.e., inside the proof block demarcated by the curly braces. Unless explicitly reintroduced, the name I will not 
refer to anything outside of that block. 

In general, names introduced inside a block { ... } are ONLY visible inside that block, not outside. 
Thus, for example:

          ... 
          assume ... {
            foo := ....
            ... # foo is fine to use here 
          };
          # But now we are outside of the scope where foo was introduced, so the name is no longer bound to anything. 
          (A & B) from foo, ... # <-- ERROR: foo IS NO LONGER VISIBLE HERE! 
      
Be very mindful of the scope of every single name in a proof. 

6. In NDL, a pound sign ("#") is used to write comments that extend to the end of the line. 
Comments should be widely used to clarify NDL proofs. 

7. Starting premises are written as follows:

assert <premise-name> := p 

where <premise-name> is an identifier (just an alphanumeric strings starting with an alphabetic letter,
typically something like 'premise-3' or 'premise-12') and p is any formula. The identifier <premise-name> 
can then be used inside the proof to refer to that premise. 

Reasoning by contradiction can be performed by combining hypothetical proofs and 'from' steps. In particular,
to prove a goal p by contradiction, it suffices to prove the conditional (~p ==> false). We can then derive p
with a 'from' step like this: 'p from (~p ==> false)'. 

The constant 'false' can be derived with a 'from' step by listing any two contradictory sentences p and (~ p) that
currently hold as justifiers:

false from p, (~ p)

Here are a couple of examples of NDL proofs:

# BEGIN_EXAMPLES

** Example 1: 

-- Input: 
assert premise-1 := ((~A & B) ==> (A & C))
assert premise-2 := B 

Derive: A 

NDL Proof:

{
  # We will proceed by contradiction. Assume the negation of the desired conclusion, (~A):
  assume (~A) {
    # From the hypothesis (~A) and the premise B, we can conclude (~A & B):
    (~A & B) from (~A), premise-2;  
    # But now we can use this with premise-1 to infer (A & C)
    (A & C)  from premise-1, (~A & B); 
    # And if we have (A & C), we clearly have A: 
    A from (A & C); 
    # But this explicitly contradicts our assumption, (~ A), so we can conclude false: 
    false from A, (~A)
  }; 
  # At this point, the preceding conditional subproof has derived the conditional (~A ==> false). 
  # Thus, reasoning by contradiction now allows us to derive our goal A:
  A from (~A ==> false) 
}

** Example 2: 

-- Input: 
assert premise-1 := A1
assert premise-2 := (~A2 ==> ~A1)
assert premise-3 := (A2 ==> A3)
assert premise-4 := (~A4 ==> ~A3)
assert premise-5 := (~A5 ==> ~A4)
assert premise-6 := (~A6 ==> ~A2 | ~A5)
assert premise-7 := (A6 & ~A7 ==> ~A3 | ~A2)
assert premise-8 := (A3 & A6 & ~A8 & A7 & A4 & A2 ==> ~A1)

Derive: A8

{
  # We can derive A2-A7 by simple incremental chaining: 
  A2 from premise-1, premise-2;
  A3 from A2, premise-3;
  A4 from A3, premise-4;
  A5 from A4, premise-5;
  A6 from A2, A5, premise-6;
  A7 from A6, A2, A3, premise-7;
  # We can now rule out a number of the alternatives listed by premise-8: 
  lemma := (~A6 | ~A7 | A8) from A1, A2, A3, A4, premise-8;
  # And by further ruling out (~A6) and (~A7), we get our goal A8: 
  A8 from lemma, A6, A7;
}
