You will be given an equational proof as a sequence of steps, and a set of equational axioms 
that collectively underwrite the proof. Your objective is to determine, for each step in the proof, 
which of the given equational axioms, if any, justify that particular step. 

The proof is given as a chain of steps that incrementally rewrite a starting term to 
an ending term. In particular, the proof consists of the following:

(1) a set EQN comprising m > 0 named equations E_1,...,E_m; and 

(2) a sequence of n+1 steps S_0, S_1, ..., S_n. Each step S_i consists simply of a term t_i, 
denoted by term(S_i). The term of the first step S_0, term(S_0), is the starting term, 
and the term of the last step, term(S_n), is the ending term.

Your objective is to do the following for each step S_i where i > 0: single out a minimal 
set of equations drawn from EQN, call that set EQN(S_i), such that the equations in EQN(S_i) 
license the passage from term(S_{i-1}) to term(S_i). That is, the equations you choose for 
EQN(S_i) must prove that the term of that step can be derived from the term of the preceding 
step by simple equational reasoning. (Exactly what is meant by "simple equational reasoning" 
will be clarified in detail shortly.) The set should be minimal in that it must not contain 
any redundant elements; every equation in it must be necessary for justifying the step in question.

If term(S_i) does not follow from term(S_{i-1}) by the sort of simple equational reasoning 
we are about to define, then you should output the empty set of equations. 

In particular, your output should be a JSON dictionary with two keys, "explanation" and 
"stepJustifications". The value of "explanation" should be a text (a plain string) that explains 
your strategy in detail - how you went about figuring out the answers for the various steps. 
If you found any step(s) particularly challenging, this is the place to discuss those challenges
and explain how you handled them. 

The value of "stepJustifications" should be  a list of n JSON dictionaries, one for each of 
the n steps in the proof: S_1, S_2, ..., S_n. Each of these n dictionaries in your output list 
should have two keys, "step" and "supportingEquations", where the value of the former is an integer 
and the value of the latter is a list of strings representing  equation names: 

[{"step": 1, "supportingEquations": <EQN(S_1), i.e., the (names of the) equations justifying the passage from term(S_0) to term(S_1)>},
 {"step": 2, "supportingEquations": <EQN(S_2), the (names of the) equations justifying the passage from term(S_1) to term(S_2)>}, 
 ...
 {"step": n, "supportingEquations": <EQN(S_n), the (names of the) equations justifying the passage from term(S_{n-1}) to term(S_n)>}]. 

Thus, given your choices for each EQN(S_i), i > 0, the full proof will be schematically 
understood as follows:

    term(S_0)
  = term(S_1)  by EQN(S_1)
  = term(S_2)  by EQN(S_2)
  ... 
  = term(S_n)  by EQN(S_n)

Here is a simple example, with g(a,f(b)) as the starting term, g(f(f(f(b))),a) as the ending term, 
and EQN comprising just two equations E1: f(X) = f(f(X) and E2: g(X,Y) = g(Y,X). Variable names start 
with upper-case letters, whereas the names of function symbols and constants start with lower-case 
letters. Every equation is understood to be universally quantified over all the variables that occur in it, 
so E2, for example, should be understood as (forall X, Y . g(X,Y) = g(Y,X)). The given proof has 4 steps: 

s = g(a,f(b))            # Step S_0
  = g(f(b),a)            # Step S_1
  = g(f(f(b)),a)         # Step S_2
  = g(f(f(f(b))),a)      # Step S_3 

In this case, your output should indicate that S_1 is underwritten by E2, S_2 by E1, 
and S_3 by E1 as well: 

s = g(a,f(b))                 # Step S_0
  = g(f(b),a)         by E2   # Step S_1
  = g(f(f(b)),a)      by E1   # Step S_2
  = g(f(f(f(b))),a)   by E1   # Step S_3 

More precisely, your output should be the following JSON dictionary:

{"explanation": <entirely up to you>,
 "stepJustifications": [{"step": 1, "supportingEquations": ["E2"]},
                        {"step": 2, "supportingEquations": ["E1"]},
                        {"step": 3, "supportingEquations": ["E1"]}]}

This means that the term of each step S_i for i > 0 can be derived from the term of the previous 
step on the basis of the equations in the "supportingEquations" list, using simple equational reasoning.
In particular: 

Step S_1: By E2, we have g(X,Y) = g(Y,X) for all X, Y. Hence, the previous term, term(S_0) = g(a,f(b)), 
can be rewritten as term(S_1) = g(f(b),a) by switching the order of the two arguments. 

Step S_2: By E1, we have f(X) = f(f(X)) for all X, and hence f(b) = f(f(b)). Thus, term(S_1) = g(f(b),a) 
can be rewritten into g(f(f(b)),a), which is precisely term(S_2). 

Step S_3: Again by E1, we have f(X) = f(f(X)) for all X, and this f(f(b)) = f(f(f(b))). Accordingly, 
term(S_2) = g(f(f(b)),a) can be rewritten into g(f(f(f(b))),a), which is term(S_3). This step concludes 
the proof successfully. 

The following signature is used for function symbols: 

Constant symbols            (arity 0): a, b, c, d, e         
Unary function symbols      (arity 1): f, f1, f2, f3, f4, f5
Binary function symbols     (arity 2): g, g1, g2, g3, g4, g5
Ternary function symbols    (arity 3): h, h1, h2, h3, h4, h5
Quaternary function symbols (arity 4): r, r1, r2, r3, r4, r5 

Note that constant symbols do not necessarily denote distinct elements. (For example, the given 
equational axioms might imply that a = d.) 

Variable names start with in upper-case letters: X, Y, V123, etc. 

Terms are essentially tree structures. Constant symbols and variables can be viewed as leaves, whereas 
a complex term like g(s,t) is a tree of height greater than 1, with the function symbol g at the root 
and the immediate subterms s and t as its subtrees. The notion of a subterm then corresponds to the notion 
of a subtree. For instance, the term t = h(a,f(b),g(c,d)) has the proper subterms a, f(b), b, g(c,d), c, and d. 
Every term is a subterm of itself, though not a proper subterm. Using Dewey-style positional notation to 
navigate trees, we may say that the root of the term t above, namely the symbol h, occurs at position []. 
At position [1] we find the constant symbol a. At position [2] we find the subterm f(b), and thus at 
position [2, 1] we find the constant symbol b. At position [3] we have g(c,d) and thus at positions [3,1] 
and [3,2] we have the constants c and d respectively. We can also associate these positions with the entire 
subterms rooted there, writing subterm(t,p) to denote the subterm of t found at position p:

subterm(t,[]) = h(a,f(b),g(c,d))
subterm(t,[1]) = a
subterm(t,[2]) = f(b)
subterm(t,[2,1]) = b 
subterm(t,[3]) = g(c,d)
subterm(t,[3,1]) = c
subterm(t,[3,2]) = d 

Note that subterm is a partial function, meaning that subterm(t,p) is undefined if p is not a 
valid position in the tree representation of t. 

We say that two positions are disjoint iff neither is a prefix of the other. 

A substitution theta is a finite function from variables to terms, e.g., theta = {V12 = a, V354 = f(b)}. 
To *apply* such a substitution theta to a term t is simply to replace every variable v in t that occurs 
in the domain of theta with the corresponding term, theta(v). For instance, applying the above substitution 
to the term s = h(a,V12, g(V12,V354)) produces the term theta(s) = h(a,a,g(a,f(b))). We say that 
a term t *matches* a term s (or is "an instance" of s) iff there is a substitution theta such that 
theta(s) = t. For example, g(X,f(b)) matches g(X,Y) under the substitution {Y = f(b)}. 

If e is an equation (s1 = s2), we write LHS(e) and RHS(e) for s1 and s2, respectively. And we say that a term s 
*reduces* or *rewrites* to a term t via e, written as s -->_e t, iff there is a position p such that subterm(s,p) 
matches LHS(e) under some substitution theta and t is identical to s except that subterm(t,p) = theta(RHS(e)). 
We refer to subterm(s,p) as a *redex* of s under e, or as an "e-redex", and we refer to subterm(t,p) as the 
corresponding *contractum*. Thus, t is obtained from s by replacing the e-redex at position p by the corresponding 
contractum. 

If E = {e_1,...,e_k} is a set of k > 0 equations, we write s ==>_E t iff s contains k distinct redexes,
one for each e_i in E, in k *disjoint* positions; and t is obtained from s by replacing each of these 
k redexes by the corresponding contractum. Thus, if s ==>_E t then we have 
s -->_{e_1} s_1 -->_{e_2} s_2 ... -->_{e_k} s_k = t for k-1 intermediate terms s_i. However, because
the k redexes occur in disjoint positions of s, we can imagine all of these reductions occurring concurrently
in one fell swoop. Note that, by definition, if s ==>_E t then E is minimal in the sense that it has no 
redundant equations; all k equations in E are used to reduce k redexes in s. 

We can now specify exactly what it means to say that a term t can be derived from a term s 
by "simple equational reasoning". It means that there is a subset E of EQN, having exactly k > 0 equations, 
such that s ==>_E t. Thus, the "supportingEquations" you list for a given step of the proof must comprise a set
E such that s ==>_E t, where t is the step's term and s is the previous step's term.

The final term is obtained by replacing these two redexes with the corresponding contractums, namely g4(f1(f5(f3(a))),d)
